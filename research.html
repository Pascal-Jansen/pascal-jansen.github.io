Research

I work toward calibrated human-AI interaction.

Embodied AI, such as automated vehicles and service robots, increasingly acts in everyday life. These systems could make roads safer, give mobility to people who cannot drive, and take over tedious work. However, their effectiveness, people’s acceptance, and safe use often depend on human experience, such as trust, mental workload, and perceived safety.

These experiences can have an appropriate range. This range may depend on the person, the AI, and the situation. For instance, human experience can be inappropriate to the AI’s actual capabilities, as in undertrust or overtrust. The AI’s behavior can also be inappropriate to the person’s preferences, as in a driving style that feels too aggressive or a service robot that approaches too closely.

Interfaces that mediate (add, remove, or modify) information might calibrate human-AI interaction. However, people differ, so one design typically cannot serve everyone. In addition, people and AI change. A person may learn how the AI behaves, and the AI’s capabilities may change with each software update.

To calibrate despite these obstacles, user interfaces should follow two principles:

Homeostatic adaptation

In physiology, homeostasis is the process by which organisms maintain vital conditions within a stable range despite a changing environment.1

1 Davies, Adaptive Homeostasis, Molecular Aspects of Medicine, 2016.

Human experience is the vital condition, and the AI and the situation are the changing environment. Ratings, physiological signals, and behavior can indicate when experience leaves its appropriate range. The interface can then mediate information until experience returns.

Hormetic doses

In toxicology, hormesis describes how controlled exposure to a stressor can stimulate adaptation, while excessive exposure can cause harm.2

2 Calabrese & Baldwin, Defining Hormesis, Hum. Exp. Toxicol., 2002.

The AI’s limits are the stressor, and the interface controls the dose. Revealing one limit at a time can move experience toward the AI’s actual capabilities, for example from overtrust to calibrated trust. Revealing all limits at once could cause harm.

Featured Work

Toward homeostatic adaptation OptiCarVis is a first step toward homeostatic adaptation. It optimizes visualizations of what an automated vehicle detects, predicts, and plans. The person’s ratings served as feedback, and an optimizer proposed each next design from them. The optimized designs improved trust, acceptance, and perceived safety without an increase in mental workload.

OptiCarVis: Improving Automated Vehicle Functionality Visualizations Using Bayesian Optimization to Enhance User Experience

Pascal Jansen, Mark Colley, Svenja Krauß, Daniel Hirschle, and Enrico Rukzio. 2025. In Proceedings of the 2025 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI ’25). Association for Computing Machinery. https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713514 Honorable Mention Award.

Toward hormetic doses This study is a first step toward hormetic doses. Its visualizations show an automated vehicle’s uncertain detections and predictions in situ. Each shows one limit of the AI at the moment it matters, a small dose. The 50 participants saw the visualizations twice a day for three days, and their trust and perceived safety increased over the three days.

Longitudinal Effects of Visualizing Uncertainty of Situation Detection and Prediction of Automated Vehicles on User Perceptions

Pascal Jansen, Mark Colley, Max Rädler, Jonas Schwedler, and Enrico Rukzio. 2025. Transportation Research Part F: Traffic Psychology and Behaviour. Elsevier. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.trf.2025.05.013

Toward the interface as mediator A mediating interface changes what the person perceives of the AI and its surroundings. MIRAGE realizes this idea for the view through the windshield. It adds, removes, and modifies objects in real time in real vehicles, built on models for object segmentation, depth estimation, and inpainting.

MIRAGE: Enabling Real-Time Automotive Mediated Reality

Pascal Jansen, Julian Britten, Mark Colley, Markus Sasalovici, and Enrico Rukzio. 2026. In Proceedings of the 2026 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI ’26). Association for Computing Machinery. https://doi.org/10.1145/3772318.3791195
